Dexterous hands are usually just graspers, and the arm does the moving. This framework flips that around: with the wrist locked, finger articulation alone drives the tweezer tip through a ±5 mm Cartesian workspace — and the hand ends up looking like a small, redundant manipulator.

Abstract

Dexterous robotic hands are commonly used to grasp tools while the gross motion of the tool is generated by the robot arm. However, human manipulation often follows a different strategy: the wrist remains approximately stationary while the fingers generate small, precise tool motions. This capability is particularly important for tools such as tweezers, forceps, needles, and scalpels, where millimeter-scale motions are frequently required.

This paper presents a model-based framework for precise in-hand manipulation of tweezers using a multi-finger dexterous hand with a stationary wrist. The proposed formulation explicitly models the kinematic relationship between finger joints, finger-tool contact points, the rigid-body motion of the tweezers, and the motion of the tweezer tips. The tweezers are further modeled as an articulated tool with one internal opening degree of freedom. A constrained inverse-kinematics controller is formulated to generate coordinated finger motions while maintaining stable contacts and satisfying joint and grasp constraints.

The framework enables Cartesian control of the tweezer tip in a small workspace, for example ±5 mm translation in arbitrary directions, without moving the robot arm. A simplified planar example illustrates the formulation. The proposed model provides a baseline for high-precision dexterous tool manipulation and can be extended with force control, tactile sensing, online model correction, and model predictive control.

02

I. Introduction

Most robotic manipulation systems treat the robotic hand primarily as a grasping mechanism. Once an object or tool has been grasped, its Cartesian motion is typically generated by moving the wrist or the robot arm.

Human manipulation is substantially more dexterous. During writing, suturing, dissection, electronic assembly, or the use of tweezers, the wrist may remain nearly stationary while several fingers coordinate to produce small tool-tip displacements.

The problem considered in this paper is therefore:

Given a dexterous robotic hand holding a pair of tweezers, can the Cartesian position and orientation of the tweezer tip be accurately controlled using finger motion alone while the robot wrist remains fixed?

The target scale considered here is local precision manipulation:

$$|\Delta x|,|\Delta y|,|\Delta z|\leq 5~\mathrm{mm}.$$

The problem is related to classical multifinger manipulation. Cole, Hauser, and Sastry developed fundamental kinematic models for multifingered manipulation with rolling contacts, establishing relationships between finger motion, contact motion, and object motion [1].

Tool manipulation using actual tweezers has also been demonstrated. Senoo et al. analyzed finger–tweezer contacts and used high-speed visual servoing to manipulate small objects with tweezers in both 2-D and 3-D environments [2].

More recently, differentiable contact models and trajectory optimization have been used for multifinger manipulation of tools such as screwdrivers, including explicit treatment of rolling contacts and complex geometry [3].

In contrast to approaches where the arm produces most of the tool motion, this work focuses specifically on local intrinsic manipulation by the hand.

The objective is to establish a simple model-based formulation that can serve as a baseline before introducing learning-based methods.

03

II. Problem Formulation

Consider a dexterous robotic hand with \(n\) actuated finger joints.

Let

$$q= [q_1,\ldots,q_n]^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$$

denote the finger joint configuration.

The wrist and robot arm are assumed to remain fixed.

The tweezers are represented by two components:

  1. a rigid-body pose of the tool,
  2. an internal opening coordinate.

Let the rigid pose be

$$T_o= \begin{bmatrix} R_o&p_o\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} \in SE(3),$$

where

$$p_o\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$$

is the tweezer reference position and

$$R_o\in SO(3)$$

is its orientation.

Its spatial velocity is

$$V_o= \begin{bmatrix} v_o\\ \omega_o \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb{R}^{6}.$$

Let

$$\alpha$$

describe the opening of the tweezers.

The generalized tool velocity is therefore

$$\nu = \begin{bmatrix} V_o\\ \dot{\alpha} \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb{R}^{7}.$$

This gives the tweezers a 6 + 1 DOF model:

$$\boxed{ \nu= [v_x,v_y,v_z, \omega_x,\omega_y,\omega_z, \dot\alpha]^T }$$

The first six variables determine where the tweezers move, while the seventh controls opening and closing.

04

III. Finger Kinematics

Suppose \(m\) fingertips contact the tweezers.

For finger \(i\),

$$p_i=f_i(q_i)$$

and its Cartesian velocity is

$$\dot p_i=J_i(q_i)\dot q_i,$$

where \(J_i\) is the ordinary finger Jacobian.

Stacking all fingers gives

$$v_c=J_h(q)\dot q,$$

where

$$J_h= \begin{bmatrix} J_1&0&\cdots\\ 0&J_2&\cdots\\ \vdots&&\ddots \end{bmatrix}.$$

Thus,

$$\boxed{ v_c=J_h(q)\dot q }$$
(1)

is simply the standard manipulator Jacobian relationship applied to multiple fingers.

The principal difference between conventional arm control and dexterous tool manipulation occurs in the next layer.

05

IV. Tool Contact Kinematics

Consider contact \(i\) between a fingertip and the tweezers.

Let the location of this contact in the tool frame be

$$r_i(\alpha).$$

Its world-frame location is

$$p_{c_i} = p_o+R_o r_i(\alpha).$$

Differentiation gives

$$\dot p_{c_i} = v_o+ \omega_o\times R_o r_i + R_o \frac{\partial r_i}{\partial\alpha} \dot\alpha.$$

This can be expressed as

$$\dot p_{c_i} = A_i(T_o,\alpha)\nu,$$

with

$$A_i= \begin{bmatrix} I & -[R_or_i]_\times & R_o\frac{\partial r_i}{\partial\alpha} \end{bmatrix}.$$

For all contacts,

$$v_c=A(T_o,\alpha)\nu.$$

Under a sticking-contact assumption, the fingertip velocity and tool contact velocity must be identical:

$$\boxed{ J_h(q)\dot q = A(T_o,\alpha)\nu }$$
(2)

Equation (2) is the central kinematic constraint of the proposed model.

For a conventional robot arm,

$$J(q)\dot q=v_{\mathrm{EE}}.$$

For the dexterous hand–tool system,

$$\boxed{ J_h(q)\dot q=A\nu. }$$

The matrix \(A\) therefore connects hand kinematics with tool kinematics.

06

V. Tweezer Tip Kinematics

The main task variable is normally not the center of the tweezers, but their tips.

Let the positions of the two tips in the tweezer frame be

$$r_t^+(\alpha),\qquad r_t^-(\alpha).$$

Their world positions are

$$p_t^\pm = p_o+R_or_t^\pm(\alpha).$$

The midpoint of the tips is

$$p_t= \frac{p_t^++p_t^-}{2}.$$

The tip separation is

$$d= \|p_t^+-p_t^-\|.$$

A useful task representation is therefore

$$y= \begin{bmatrix} p_t\\ \phi_o\\ d \end{bmatrix},$$

where

  • \(p_t\in\mathbb{R}^3\) is the tool-tip midpoint,
  • \(\phi_o\in\mathbb{R}^3\) represents tool orientation,
  • \(d\in\mathbb{R}\) represents tweezer opening.

Thus

$$y\in\mathbb{R}^7.$$

Differentiating,

$$\boxed{ \dot y=H(T_o,\alpha)\nu. }$$
(3)

This provides a direct tool-task representation:

$$\text{finger joints} \rightarrow \text{contacts} \rightarrow \text{tool motion} \rightarrow \text{tweezer tips}.$$
07

VI. Model-Based Cartesian Controller

Suppose the desired task is

$$y_d.$$

Define the task error

$$e=y_d-y.$$

A Cartesian velocity command can be constructed as

$$\dot y_d^* = \dot y_d+ K_pe.$$

For a static target,

$$\dot y_d=0,$$

giving

$$\dot y_d^*=K_p(y_d-y).$$

The controller must find finger joint velocities \(\dot q\) and tool velocity \(\nu\) satisfying

$$J_h\dot q=A\nu$$

while generating the desired task motion

$$H\nu\approx\dot y_d^*.$$

A natural formulation is quadratic programming:

$$\boxed{ \begin{aligned} \min_{\dot q,\nu}\quad& \|H\nu-\dot y_d^*\|_{W_t}^{2} + \lambda_q\|\dot q\|^2 + \lambda_\nu\|\nu\|^2\\ \text{s.t.}\quad& J_h(q)\dot q=A(T_o,\alpha)\nu\\ &q_{\min}\leq q+\dot q\Delta t\leq q_{\max}\\ &\dot q_{\min}\leq\dot q\leq\dot q_{\max}. \end{aligned} }$$
(4)

Equation (4) is the proposed basic controller.

It has several advantages over directly computing a pseudoinverse.

It naturally handles:

  • redundant finger DOFs,
  • joint limits,
  • velocity limits,
  • preferred hand postures,
  • multiple simultaneous task objectives,
  • orientation constraints,
  • tweezer opening constraints.

Additional objectives can easily be added.

For example,

$$\lambda_p\|q-q_{\mathrm{nominal}}\|^2$$

can keep the hand near a comfortable grasp posture.

08

VII. Planar Example

Consider a simplified two-dimensional example.

Two fingers hold a rigid tweezer whose opening remains fixed.

Each finger has two revolute joints:

$$q= [q_{11},q_{12},q_{21},q_{22}]^T.$$

The planar configuration of the tweezers is

$$\xi= [x,y,\theta]^T.$$

Assume that the two contact points relative to the tool center are

$$r_1= \begin{bmatrix} -a\\0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad r_2= \begin{bmatrix} a\\0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

For \(\theta=0\), the velocity of contact 1 is

$$\dot p_1= \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\ 0&1&-a \end{bmatrix} \dot\xi,$$

while contact 2 satisfies

$$\dot p_2= \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\ 0&1&a \end{bmatrix} \dot\xi.$$

Therefore,

$$A= \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\ 0&1&-a\\ 1&0&0\\ 0&1&a \end{bmatrix}.$$

The complete system satisfies

$$\boxed{ J_h(q)\dot q=A\dot\xi. }$$
(5)

A. Translating the Tweezers by 5 mm

Suppose the required motion is

$$\Delta \xi= \begin{bmatrix} 5~\mathrm{mm}\\ 0\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Then

$$A\Delta\xi= \begin{bmatrix} 5\\ 0\\ 5\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\mathrm{mm}.$$

Therefore both fingertip contacts must move 5 mm in the positive \(x\) direction:

$$\Delta p_1= \Delta p_2= \begin{bmatrix} 5\\0 \end{bmatrix}\mathrm{mm}.$$

The required finger displacement can be found from

$$J_h\Delta q=A\Delta\xi.$$

For a redundant hand,

$$\Delta q= J_h^\dagger A\Delta\xi + (I-J_h^\dagger J_h)\Delta q_0.$$

The second term lies in the null space and can be used to maintain a desirable finger configuration without affecting the desired tool motion.

B. Pure Rotation

Suppose instead that

$$\Delta\xi= \begin{bmatrix} 0\\0\\\Delta\theta \end{bmatrix}.$$

Then

$$\Delta p_1= \begin{bmatrix} 0\\-a\Delta\theta \end{bmatrix},$$

and

$$\Delta p_2= \begin{bmatrix} 0\\a\Delta\theta \end{bmatrix}.$$

Thus one finger moves upward while the other moves downward.

The rotation of the tweezers emerges naturally from coordinated fingertip motion.

This illustrates an important property of dexterous manipulation:

Cartesian tool motion can be generated from combinations of relatively small coordinated finger motions.

VIII. Grasp Force Model

Kinematic control alone does not guarantee that the tweezers remain securely grasped.

Let

$$f= [f_1^T,\ldots,f_m^T]^T$$

represent fingertip contact forces.

The resulting tool wrench is

$$w=Gf,$$

where \(G\) is the grasp matrix.

Under quasi-static conditions,

$$Gf+w_{\mathrm{ext}}=0.$$

Finger torques are related to contact forces by

$$\boxed{ \tau=J_h^Tf. }$$
(6)

A particularly useful component of the contact force is the internal grasp force:

$$f_{\mathrm{int}}\in\mathrm{Null}(G).$$

Since

$$Gf_{\mathrm{int}}=0,$$

these forces can increase grasp stability without generating net motion of the tweezers.

The total force can therefore be represented as

$$f=f_{\mathrm{task}}+f_{\mathrm{int}}.$$

This naturally separates two objectives:

$$\boxed{ \text{finger motion} \rightarrow \text{tool-tip motion} }$$

and

$$\boxed{ \text{internal finger force} \rightarrow \text{grasp stability}. }$$

The concept follows classical multifinger grasp and coordinated manipulation formulations [1,4].

10

IX. Integrated Motion–Force Optimization

The kinematic controller can therefore be extended to simultaneously optimize motion and grasp force:

$$\begin{aligned} \min_{\dot q,\nu,f}\quad& \|H\nu-\dot y_d^*\|^2_{W_y}\\ &+\lambda_q\|\dot q\|^2\\ &+\lambda_f\|f-f_{\mathrm{nom}}\|^2\\ \text{s.t.}\quad& J_h\dot q=A\nu,\\ &Gf+w_{\mathrm{ext}}=0,\\ &f_i\in\mathcal{F}_i,\\ &\tau=J_h^Tf,\\ &\tau_{\min}\leq\tau\leq\tau_{\max}. \end{aligned}$$
(7)

Here,

$$\mathcal{F}_i$$

denotes the friction cone of contact \(i\).

Equation (7) gives a complete model-based formulation for dexterous tweezer manipulation.

X. Proposed Experimental System

A practical system can consist of:

  • a multi-DOF dexterous robotic hand,
  • joint encoders,
  • fingertip force or tactile sensors,
  • a conventional pair of tweezers,
  • an external optical tracker or camera observing the tool,
  • a fixed robot wrist.

The arm first brings the hand near the operating region.

The arm is then locked.

All subsequent fine manipulation is produced by finger motion.

A useful benchmark workspace is

$$[-5,5]\times[-5,5]\times[-5,5]~\mathrm{mm}.$$

The experimental sequence can contain six basic translations:

$$(\pm5,0,0),$$
$$(0,\pm5,0),$$
$$(0,0,\pm5)\ \mathrm{mm}.$$

More complex trajectories can subsequently be evaluated:

  1. a 5-mm-radius circle,
  2. a square trajectory,
  3. a 3-D helix,
  4. point-to-point positioning,
  5. grasping a small object using tweezer opening and closing.

Performance should be evaluated using

$$e_p= \|p_t-p_t^d\|,$$

orientation error

$$e_R,$$

and opening error

$$e_d=|d-d_d|.$$

Important metrics include:

  • RMS tool-tip tracking error,
  • maximum tracking error,
  • repeatability,
  • orientation error,
  • grasp-force variation,
  • contact slip,
  • successful grasp rate.

A particularly meaningful first target is

$$\mathrm{RMSE}_{tip}<1~\mathrm{mm}.$$

XI. Discussion

The formulation highlights a useful distinction between arm manipulation and dexterous in-hand manipulation.

For conventional arm manipulation,

$$\dot x=J_{\mathrm{arm}}\dot q_{\mathrm{arm}}.$$

For dexterous tool manipulation,

$$\dot q_{\mathrm{finger}} \rightarrow v_{\mathrm{contact}} \rightarrow V_{\mathrm{tool}} \rightarrow v_{\mathrm{tip}}.$$

Consequently, the effective Jacobian is not merely the Jacobian of an individual finger.

It is a hand–tool task Jacobian.

Conceptually,

$$\boxed{ J_{\mathrm{task}} = J_{\mathrm{tip/tool}} J_{\mathrm{contact/tool}}^\dagger J_{\mathrm{finger}}. }$$

This allows the dexterous hand to be interpreted as a miniature redundant manipulator whose effective end effector is the tip of the tool rather than the fingertip.

Such an interpretation may be particularly useful in tasks where the robot arm provides large-workspace positioning while the fingers provide a second, much smaller but higher-precision manipulation workspace.

This creates a natural hierarchical architecture:

$$\text{Arm} \rightarrow \text{coarse positioning}$$

followed by

$$\text{Dexterous hand} \rightarrow \text{fine positioning}.$$

The approach is complementary to more complex trajectory-optimization approaches that explicitly model rolling and changing finger-object contacts [3].

13

XII. Limitations and Extensions

The initial model assumes known geometry and sufficiently stable finger-tool contacts.

Several extensions are important.

A. Rolling Contact

Finger contact points may move along the tweezer surface.

In this case,

$$r_i=r_i(t)$$

must become an additional state, and rolling constraints should be introduced. Classical rolling-contact formulations [1] and recent differentiable rolling models [3] provide suitable foundations.

B. Contact Slip

If sticking cannot be guaranteed, frictional contact modes should explicitly distinguish

$$\text{stick},\quad \text{roll},\quad \text{slide}.$$

C. Tweezer Compliance

Real tweezers are elastic rather than ideal revolute mechanisms.

A more accurate model can introduce

$$\tau_\alpha = k_\alpha(\alpha-\alpha_0) + b_\alpha\dot\alpha.$$

Thus the tweezer opening becomes both a geometric and mechanical state.

D. Model Predictive Control

Equation (7) can naturally be extended from instantaneous QP control to model predictive control:

$$\min_{\{q_k,\nu_k,f_k\}_{k=0}^{N}} \sum_{k=0}^{N} \|y_k-y_k^d\|_Q^2 + \|\dot q_k\|_R^2.$$

This allows joint limits, future contact configurations, and grasp stability to be considered over a finite horizon.

14

XIII. Conclusion

This paper presents a model-based formulation for dexterous manipulation of tweezers using finger motion alone.

The principal relationship is

$$\boxed{ J_h(q)\dot q=A(T_o,\alpha)\nu, }$$

which connects dexterous-hand joint motion to the rigid and articulated motion of the tweezers.

The tool task is described by

$$\boxed{ \dot y=H(T_o,\alpha)\nu, }$$

where \(y\) contains the position and orientation of the tweezer tip as well as its opening.

Together, these equations transform dexterous tool use into a constrained inverse-kinematics problem similar in spirit to conventional robot-arm Cartesian control, while accounting for multiple finger contacts and the internal degrees of freedom of the tool.

The resulting model suggests a simple but useful manipulation architecture: the robot arm provides coarse positioning, while the dexterous hand generates the final millimeter-scale motion of the tool.

For applications such as tweezer manipulation, microsurgical tools, laboratory manipulation, and precision assembly, this division of manipulation between arm and fingers may provide both increased dexterity and higher local positioning accuracy.
